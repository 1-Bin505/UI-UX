Tihar & Chhath

Tihar: A five-day festival of light, lamps, kinship and reverence—honouring crows, dogs, cows, Laxmi, the self and siblings.

Chhath: A sun-and-river observance dedicated to Surya and Chhathi Maiya, marked by devotion, fasts and offerings at dusk and dawn.

Tihar · Day 1

Kaag Tihar

The first day of Tihar honours the crow, a bird that is believed to carry messages between the living and the world of the dead.

What is done
Families put out grains, sweets and other food on rooftops, balconies and doorsteps for the crows, and many offer prayers as they do so.
Why
The crow is seen as a messenger of Yama, the god of death. Its harsh call is taken as a sign of coming sorrow, so feeding it is a way of keeping grief away from the household.
Short history
Tihar, also called Yamapanchak (“five days of Yama”), opens with this day. Its animal worship grows out of old Hindu ideas about Yama and his attendants, and the festival is now Nepal’s second biggest after Dashain.

Tihar · Day 2

Kukur Tihar

On the second day, every dog, whether a family pet or a stray on the street, is treated as an honoured guest.

What is done
Dogs receive a red tika on the forehead, a garland of marigolds around the neck and a plate of tasty food. The Newar community calls this Khicha Puja.
Why
It is a thank-you for the dog’s loyalty and watchfulness, and a way of acknowledging the bond between people and dogs.
Short history
Dogs hold a special place in Hindu mythology as guardians linked with Yama and the gates of the afterlife. In recent years, photos of garlanded dogs have spread across the internet and made this day famous well beyond Nepal.

Tihar · Day 3 (morning)

Gai Puja

Day three begins by thanking the cow, the animal that feeds the household and the fields.

What is done
Cows are washed, given a tika and a garland, and fed good grass and treats. Homes are cleaned, and doorways are often smeared with a mixture of cow dung and red clay.
Why
The cow is regarded as an earthly form of Laxmi, the goddess of wealth, because its milk and dung nourish people and farmland. Honouring it prepares the home for Laxmi’s visit that evening.
Short history
Reverence for the cow runs through Hindu tradition for thousands of years. In Tihar it forms the morning half of a double day: Gai Tihar by daylight, Laxmi Puja after dark.

Tihar · Day 3 (evening)

Laxmi Puja

At dusk, every window glows. Lamps are lit so that the goddess of wealth finds a clean, bright home to enter.

What is done
Houses are swept and decorated with oil lamps, candles and lights. Rangoli patterns in coloured powder lead from the door to the prayer room, and families worship Laxmi with flowers, incense and sweets.
Why
Laxmi is believed to visit homes that are tidy and lit, bringing wealth and good fortune for the year ahead. The light also stands for good over darkness.
Short history
Laxmi is said to have risen from the churning of the cosmic ocean as the goddess of prosperity. Tihar is the Nepali form of Deepawali, the festival of lights, and this night is its centrepiece.

Tihar · Day 4

Mha Puja

The fourth day has several faces. For the Newars of Kathmandu Valley it is a night to honour the self.

What is done
In the evening, Newar families draw a mandala with oil, coloured powder and rice, place lamps, flowers and fruit on it, and worship the body and inner soul of each family member. Elsewhere, people worship oxen (Goru Puja) or make a hill of cow dung for Govardhan Puja.
Why
Mha Puja treats the person as a sacred being worth cleansing and blessing, so that the year can start with a healthy body and mind.
Short history
The day usually falls on the first day of Nepal Sambat, the Newar lunar calendar, which makes it a New Year celebration. Govardhan Puja recalls the story of Krishna lifting a hill to shelter people and cattle from a flood.

Tihar · Day 5

Bhai Tika

The last day of Tihar is for siblings. Sisters bless their brothers and pray for their long, healthy lives.

What is done
Brothers sit on the floor while sisters trace a line of oil around them from a copper pitcher, oil their hair, and apply a multi-coloured tika with a garland. Gifts, fruit, sweets and blessings are then exchanged.
Why
The rite is a sister’s protective prayer for her brother and a celebration of the family bond. The oil circle is meant to shield him from harm, including the reach of death.
Short history
A popular legend says that the river goddess Yamuna invited her brother Yama, the god of death, and honoured him with tika and food, after which he granted that brothers blessed this way would live long. The day falls on the second day of the bright fortnight of Kartik.

Tihar · Nights of Laxmi Puja and Bhai Tika

Deusi-Bhailo

After dark, groups of carolers go from house to house with madal drums, songs and dance. Give dakshina, and the song begins again.

What is done
Boys and men sing Deusi, and girls and women sing Bhailo. A lead singer calls out the verses, the group answers, and each visit ends with a blessing for the household. Hosts give money, fruit or rice bread in return.
Why
The songs carry good wishes for prosperity and health into every home. The gifts reward the singers, and the money is often used for community projects.
Short history
Folk tradition connects the custom to the legend of King Bali, who was humbled by Vishnu in the form of Vamana. It remains one of the liveliest, most community-minded parts of Tihar.

Chhath · Day 3

Sandhya Arghya

At dusk, devotees stand in the water and offer arghya to the setting sun. Press the button and watch the devotee pour the water toward Surya.

What is done
After a fast of roughly 36 hours without food or water, families walk to a river or pond carrying bamboo trays (soop) of fruit and thekua. Devotees stand in the water, offer water or milk to the setting sun, and sing Chhath songs through the evening.
Why
The festival thanks Surya, the sun, for sustaining all life, and honours Chhathi Maiya, who is asked to protect children and grant them long life and good health. Offering to the setting sun is unusual, as many solar rites begin at sunrise.
Short history
Sun worship is very old in the Indian subcontinent, and Chhathi Maiya appears in the Brahma Vaivarta Purana, which tells of the childless King Priyavrata. Chhath is especially strong in Mithila, including Janakpur in Nepal, and in Bihar and eastern Uttar Pradesh. It falls on Kartik Shukla Shashthi, six days after Diwali.

Chhath · Day 4

Usha Arghya

After a night of vigil, devotees return before dawn. The soop, heaped with thekua, fruit and sugarcane, is raised toward the rising sun.

What is done
Before sunrise, families gather again at the water. Devotees stand facing east, offer arghya to the rising sun, and share the prasad. The fast is then broken (parana), usually with the offered food.
Why
This offering closes the vow. It thanks the sun for the renewal of the day and asks for health, prosperity and the wellbeing of children from Chhathi Maiya.
Short history
Chhath is described as one of the oldest sun festivals still observed. Different stories credit its origin to figures such as Sita in Mithila and Draupadi and Karna from the Mahabharata. Devotees keep the traditional rules strict: pure vegetarian food, no onion or garlic, and a clean kitchen.
